Sickest Man Alive Character brief 01 Status: draft 2026-08-10

The main
character

A teenager, around 16 years old, in an “old tech” suit that reads as a mix of space exploration and medical equipment. Liquid capsules ride on the back and feed the syringe gun through tubes. They also carry an oversized scalpel as a sword.

Gender is the artist's call. Male or female — decide it and draw it. No competing pitches needed.

The game's visual language is still being worked out with the artist, so this brief is self-contained and is the only reference that counts for now.

Halfway between cartoon and realistic

The style target
Cartoon
key art Realistic
suit reference

Not the chunky big-headed cartoon of the key art, and not rendered realism either. Land between them: believable human proportions, stylised and simplified surfaces. Anatomy and suit hardware behave like real objects with real weight; the drawing of them stays bold, clean and exaggerated where exaggeration helps a shape read.

ref/concept/ is the anchor for character, tone and colour — not for the suit panelling, which is being redesigned. ref/suit/ is the anchor for how hardware sits on a body. The target sits between the two in rendering as well as in proportion.

The suit is already worn in. Not damaged, and it does not degrade further during a run — it is simply not new. Scuffed edges, chipped paint, stained fabric, worn straps and scratched glass from the first frame the player sees.

Reads at a glance

Survives at gameplay size
  1. A readable face. The face is the character. Partial coverage is welcome — see the headgear note — but the person has to stay visible in it.
  2. Back capsules and the tubes feeding the syringe gun. One piece of silhouette that explains the entire kit.
  3. Old tech, not sci-fi. Bulky, patched, analogue: gauges, straps, buckles, rubber hose — and scuffed before the run starts.

Play the prototype first

Before drawing anything

A playable build ships alongside this brief — Windows, or a browser version. It is programmer art from top to bottom: every shape in it is placeholder, including the character. The point is how the game moves, not how it looks.

What to watch for
  • How big the character reads against the enemies and the room.
  • How much screen the weapons and their effects take up.
  • Whether a fight stays readable with six things on screen.
  • Where the eye goes — the character, or the thing about to hit them.

Headgear

Partial coverage, ideally modular

The face does not have to be fully bare. Some coverage is welcome, and the best answer is coverage that can come on and off — a breathing mask that hangs at the chin and pulls up, goggles that sit on the forehead and drop over the eyes, a hood, a filter, a visor on a hinge.

Design it as separate modular pieces on their own layers, so a piece can be worn or stowed without redrawing the head. That also gives us an obvious place to hang item upgrades later.

The one rule

The person stays readable. Whatever is worn, the player can still see who is inside it and read an expression. A fully sealed, anonymous helmet is the failure — not coverage itself.

References

Read the ignore list first

Every reference below is partly right and partly wrong. The ignore list is the more important half — a reference handed over without one gets copied whole, including the parts that are wrong.

Avoid

ref/avoid — the wrong character entirely

Do not

  • No fully sealed helmet or anonymous full-face mask. Partial coverage is fine and wanted; coverage that hides the person is not. Every reference pulls toward the faceless trooper — do not follow it.
  • No sleek modern powered armour. Moulded plates, clean panel lines, glowing inserts and gym-built anatomy are the wrong century and the wrong body.
  • No adult heroic proportions. They are sixteen — and not cartoon-chibi either. See the style target above.
  • No factory-fresh suit. It is worn in from the first frame.
  • No weapon painted into the body art. The loadout changes both hands — see below.
  • No saturated colour on the character. Items recolour the player through a tint that a saturated design would swallow.
  • No clean surfaces. Scuffs, stains, chipped paint, worn straps.

Weapons drive the design

Interchangeable and modular

The player picks their own loadout, and all three combinations have to be drawable from the same body. Both hands are generic grips, and the character is authored holding nothing.

  • MeleeTwo scalpel swords
  • MixedScalpel sword + syringe gun
  • RangedTwo syringe guns

Item upgrades and modifiers change how the weapons look as well as what they do, so each of these is an independently swappable piece:

  • fluid colour
  • scalpel handle
  • scalpel tip / blade

Tubes run from the back capsules to whichever weapon is held, so plan the attachment path rather than drawing tubes into one fixed pose.

Technical requirements

Not preferences
RequirementDetail
LayeringEvery body part on its own layer, ready to be rigged to an animation skeleton. Delivered layered, not flattened.
BackgroundTransparent, with careful cropping — no stray pixels, no halo, no visual noise around the silhouette.
FormatPNG with straight (non-premultiplied) alpha, plus the layered source file.
ViewFront-facing. Aim direction is carried by the weapon, not the body.
WeaponsSeparate assets, with the grip point and the tube attachment point marked.
ModularityWeapon pieces — fluid, handle, tip — on their own layers, and any headgear (mask, goggles, visor) as its own layer in both its worn and its stowed position.

Rig contract

  • torso
  • head
  • arm_l
  • arm_r
  • leg_l
  • leg_r
  • + one weapon attachment per hand

Joints must be visible and unambiguous — shoulders, hips, base of the neck. Limbs must not overlap each other; limb-over-torso overlap is wanted, because it hides the seam when the limb rotates.
